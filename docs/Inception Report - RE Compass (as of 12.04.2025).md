# **Risk, expansion, and governance gaps in the Philippine energy transition** 

**The Philippines is entering its energy transition from an extremely fragile starting point**. The 2025 [World Risk Report](https://www.ifhv.de/publications/world-risk-report) again [ranks the country first worldwide](https://mb.com.ph/2025/09/26/philippines-tops-worldriskindex-2025-as-worlds-most-disaster-prone-country) in disaster risk among 193 countries, driven by high exposure to storms, floods, and other extreme events and by structural social vulnerability.  Recent analysis cited in [REI’s own case-for-action report](https://library.fes.de/pdf-files/bueros/philippinen/20989.pdf) notes that climate impacts could impose economic losses of up to 7.6 % of GDP by 2030 and 13.6% by 2040 if left unmanaged.

**Yet the power system that underpins this vulnerable context remains heavily fossil-fuel-dependent:** [coal provided almost 62 percent of electricity generation in 2023](https://www.reuters.com/markets/commodities/philippines-dependency-coal-fired-power-surpasses-china-indonesia-2024-07-01), one of the highest coal shares in Southeast Asia, despite government targets to raise renewables to 35% of the power mix by 2030 and 50 percent by 2040\. ￼ **The opportunity is also significant.** The World Bank estimates about 178 GW of technical offshore wind potential, and a high-growth scenario where offshore wind alone could supply around 14–21% of electricity by 2040 while generating jobs and value-add across the economy. ￼

**These realities—extreme climate risk, deep dependence on coal, and a rapidly expanding pipeline of “green” investments—are converging on governance systems that are still trying to catch up.** On paper, the Philippines already has a dense architecture for approving energy projects, as seen in the [Philippine Environmental Impact Statement System (PEISS)](https://ap.fftc.org.tw/article/1194) which requires environmental impact assessments (EIA) and Environmental Compliance Certificates (ECCs) for environmentally critical projects and areas. Sector-specific rules and DENR guidance are evolving to cover renewable and offshore projects more explicitly. However, the same REI report highlights that as renewables scale up, these generate serious ecological and social risks, from [livelihood threats](https://climatetracker.asia/fishers-fear-for-future-as-floating-solar-threatens-to-disrupt-philippines-largest-lake/) and [land-use conflicts](https://www.lrcksk.org/post/sipa-2022-report-launched-half-of-ancestral-domains-found-under-environmental-threat), to [biodiversity impacts](https://philchm.ph/wp-content/uploads/Documentation-RTD-on-Mainstreaming-BD-in-the-Energy-Sector-v2.pdf) and [opaque supply chains](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2025/01/philippines-nickel-mining-projects-approved-despite-inadequate-consultation-and-serious-risks-to-communities-health-and-environment/)—and that public, corporate, and civic governance mechanisms to manage these risks [remain fragmented and incomplete.](https://legacy.doe.gov.ph/sites/default/files/pdf/announcements/OSW%20Guidebook.pdf) 

**In practice, rules meant to protect biodiversity and communities in the Philippines are still applied [mostly one project at a time](https://r9.emb.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/EIA_Basic_Concepts_Process_2016April.pdf).** They depend on project-level EIAs and ECCs under the Philippine Environmental Impact Statement System (PEISS) set up by [Presidential Decree No. 1586\.](https://emb.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/PD-1586.pdf) Newer efforts, like DENR’s [biodiversity-focused strategic assessments](https://www.philchm.ph/wp-content/uploads/BDSEA.pdf) and a [proposed law to require broader environmental assessment](https://ispweb.pcaarrd.dost.gov.ph/house-approves-environmental-assessment-system-bill/)s for plans and programmes, not just projects—are trying to fill this gap. Parallel work locally is starting to use [geospatial decision-support tools](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/11/24/11955?) for just energy planning and climate-resilient infrastructure, using GIS and multi-criteria analysis to compare options and identify more suitable, locally appropriate sites and systems. These kinds of tools can help planners and communities make trade-offs more explicit and avoid locking investments into locations or project configurations that are socially or ecologically high-risk. 

However, taken together, these show how much work remains to ensure safeguards are woven into energy and infrastructure planning across whole landscapes and regions. 

It is into this pressure point that the FES–WeSolve collaboration on the initial RE Compass is positioned to prototype a simple spatial tool that pulls together project pipelines, social and ecological sensitivities, in one platform. The aim is to support more accountable siting decisions (where and how energy projects are built) and more informed engagements with government, communities and investors—and, over time, to strengthen the responsibility dimension of the Philippine energy transition, not just its speed.

# **Purpose and policy objective**

The RE Transition Map is a joint initiative of FES and WeSolve Foundation to make visible how renewable energy (RE) expansion in the Philippines intersects with land, ecosystems, and communities in the Philippines.

In this first phase (until April 2026), the purpose is modest and practical:

* to prototype a simple internal spatial tool that helps FES and WeSolve to visualize where RE projects and pipelines are located; and  
* understand how these locations overlap with a small set of social and ecological sensitivities, using data that is already available or realistically obtainable in the short term.

The immediate objective is to give FES and WeSolve a communication tool they can use to:

* anchor discussions with agencies such as DOE, DENR, NCIP and selected LGUs in specific places and patterns, rather than only in general principles; and  
* inform internal strategy conversations with civil society partners on where to look more closely, given limited time and resources.

Over the longer term, lessons from this initial prototype can feed into broader work under RE Compass and the [Responsible Energy Initiative (REI) Philippines](https://philippines.fes.de/rei.html)￼ on strengthening the “responsibility” dimension of the Philippine energy transition—understood in line with REI’s call for renewable energy that is **ecologically safe and socially just** and with existing commitments in the [Sustainable Development Goals,](https://www.un.org/sustainabledevelopment/sustainable-development-goals/) particularly Goals 7, 8, 10, 13 and 15￼, the Philippine Development Plan 2023–2028￼, and the [Philippines NDC Implementation Plan￼](https://niccdies.climate.gov.ph/files/documents/The%20Philippines-%20NDC%20Implementation%20Plan%20-%20072024.pdf) on a green and just transition. In practical terms, this means making it easier to see how risks and benefits are distributed, whose lands and livelihoods are affected, and how these realities are reflected in planning and investment decisions.

# **Priority Phase 1 use cases**

### To keep the scope focused and feasible, Phase 1 will center on two use cases:

|  Case 1 – Internal briefing aid for policy dialogues | Case 2 – Early “areas of interest” scanning for CSO engagement |
| :---- | :---- |
| A basic internal map that FES and WeSolve can use to prepare for, and selectively present in, meetings with DOE, DENR, NCIP, LGUs, and other counterparts, in order to: see a quick picture of existing and planned RE projects or pipelines by technology and location; and switch on 1–2 context layers (for example, protected areas or ancestral domains, depending on data and handling norms). | An internal view that helps FES and WeSolve: spot clusters or “areas of interest” where RE build-out overlaps with higher social or ecological sensitivity \- for example, ancestral domains and Indigenous communities, dense fishing or farming areas where people rely directly on land and water for their income, or protected areas and key biodiversity sites where habitats are already under pressure use this as a starting list for deciding where deeper case work, consultation, or monitoring might be needed. |
| Here, the map works as a briefing aid – a simple way to ground questions about siting, safeguards, and governance in specific places. | In this use case, the tool is mainly for prioritising and setting the agenda, not for making final judgments about projects. |

# **Collaboration for this phase**

* FES Philippines will provide strategic direction, ensure alignment with RE Compass and REI work, facilitate access to relevant datasets and partners, and test the prototype in at least one appropriate policy or strategy setting.  
* WeSolve Foundation will lead on concept and framing, coordinate the initial data and mapping work with the chosen developer, and support FES in applying the tool in concrete conversations, documenting key insights and limitations. The developer will implement the minimal technical build required for the prototype and provide basic documentation on how it works.  
* The Developer will implement minimal technical build for the prototype and provide basic documentation on data handling and system functionality.

More detailed role definitions, timelines, and budget breakdowns will be set out in the Agreement and Terms of Reference, and can evolve as learning emerges from prototype development.
